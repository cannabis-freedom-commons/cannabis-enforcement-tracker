Since January 2016, police officials have defended their aerial spraying of
dagga by citing an "expert report" claiming glyphosate spraying has "technically
zero" chance of harming people or animals. [@1] Researchers from the University
of Cape Town and activists disputed the findings and questioned the credentials
and independence of the report's author, Dr Gerhard Verdoorn of the Griffon
Poison Information Centre. [@1]

The South African Police Service says it has used helicopters to spray marijuana
plantations with glyphosate for more than three decades, and that the programme
is lawful and complies with local safety and environmental regulations. [@1]
Villagers in the Eastern Cape who grow marijuana for an income say the spraying
poses a health risk and infringes on basic human rights. [@1] Verdoorn's
three-page report states that it is physically impossible for any organism to be
exposed to sufficient glyphosate during police spraying operations. [@1] The
report concludes that the exposure warrants no concerns about acute toxicity,
dermal irritation or even eye irritation. [@1]

Verdoorn is a 57-year-old qualified organic chemist and former conservation
biology professor who has run the Griffon Poison Information Centre alone as its
director since 2005. [@1] Verdoorn chairs two CropLife SA committees, covering
public relations and safety, health and environment, and is a regular consultant
for AVCASA, handling its waste management programme. [@1] The president of
CropLife SA, Marcel Dreyer, is also chief executive of Arysta LifeScience, which
holds the patent for KiloMax, the glyphosate product police use to eradicate
dagga plantations. [@1] Helen Whitehead of the Transkei Animal Welfare
Initiative, one of the groups campaigning against the spraying, said Verdoorn
was not independent and was strongly financially motivated to defend glyphosate.
[@1] Verdoorn said the Griffon Centre was totally independent and entirely
self-funded. [@1] In 2015 Villa Crop Protection, one of South Africa's largest
agricultural remedy suppliers, funded toxicological analyses at the Griffon
Centre after 66 endangered vultures died from poisoning. [@1] Verdoorn said the
Villa Crop funding was once-off and that the company helps out when there is a
poisoning incident. [@1]

Verdoorn said glyphosate was the least toxic compound the police could use and
that the police were acting within their constitutional mandate. [@1] Verdoorn
called allegations of poisoning as a consequence of the spraying absolute
rubbish. [@1] Dr Leslie London, professor of public health at UCT, said Verdoorn
had a long record of interpreting findings in ways that exonerate the pesticide
industry of responsibility for health problems from pesticide exposure. [@1]
London, a B-rated NRF researcher recognised internationally for his work on
pesticides, said the report was poor science and more closely resembled a
propaganda piece. [@1] Lieutenant General E Mawela wrote to attorney Ricky Stone
on 7 March about the expert report. [@1] Mawela said the SAPS maintains it is
conducting lawful operations. [@1] The toxicologist Dr Ockie Fourie, whom the
report suggested for a second opinion, said he did a lot of consulting for the
police roughly between 1995 and 1998, monitoring their glyphosate spraying
programme. [@1] Fourie said the spraying he monitored was very scientific with
practically zero negative impact on health or the environment, but he had no
idea what had happened since because it had been many years since he worked on
glyphosate. [@1] SAPS officials did not answer GroundUp's written questions
before publication. [@1] Daily Maverick published the GroundUp article on 6 May
2016, crediting GroundUp. [@2]
