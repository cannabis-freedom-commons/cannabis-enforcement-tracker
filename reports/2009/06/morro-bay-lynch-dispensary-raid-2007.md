Charles Lynch opened Central Coast Compassionate Caregivers in Morro Bay on 1
April 2006 with a city business license, paying taxes and checking physician
referrals under California's Compassionate Use Act. [@1,2] The city attorney had
approved the licence, the police chief inspected the shop, and the mayor came to
its ribbon-cutting ceremony. [@1] Before opening, Lynch had contacted the Drug
Enforcement Administration, which told him it was up to cities and counties to
decide about dispensaries. [@2] The shop ran for almost a year without major
problems or complaints. [@1,2]

On 29 March 2007 the local sheriff and DEA agents raided the dispensary and
Lynch's home in full paramilitary attire. [@1,2] They seized his medicine and
money, including a backpack containing $27,000 described as possible proceeds
from recent sales. [@2,3] Lynch was not arrested in the raid. [@1,2] Sheriff Pat
Hedges had spent eleven months investigating the dispensary and, unable to get a
search warrant from state courts, had turned to the DEA. [@2]

Lynch reopened the dispensary on 7 April 2007 with the approval of the city.
[@1,2] A week later the DEA threatened his landlord with forfeiture of the
property unless he evicted the shop, and on 16 May 2007 the dispensary closed
permanently. [@1,2] Two months later agents arrested Lynch at his home and
charged him with five federal marijuana counts. [@2] A federal jury, which could
not hear about California's medical marijuana law, convicted him on all counts in
August 2008. [@2]

On 11 June 2009 Judge George H. Wu of the federal court in Los Angeles sentenced
Lynch, 47, to a year and a day in prison, staring straight ahead as the sentence
was imposed. [@4] Prosecutors had sought the five-year mandatory minimum and
portrayed him as a common dealer who sold more than $2 million of marijuana,
including repeated sales to people under 21; his lawyers had been barred from
arguing medical necessity for seriously ill patients. [@4] Applying the safety
valve, the judge found Lynch posed no greater danger to the public than the
dispensary's other participants and added four years of supervised release.
[@5]

Lynch appealed, and in 2012 Americans for Safe Access filed an amicus brief
supporting him, noting the administration still sought a five-year term while
the judge weighed its declarations of tolerance toward medical marijuana.
[@6]
