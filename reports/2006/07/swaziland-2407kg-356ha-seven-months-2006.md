A unit of 30 police officers trekked 5 km through rugged terrain in the northern
Hhohho region and reached a clearing planted with cannabis. [@1] The clearing
held hundreds of cannabis plants over six feet tall, known locally as dagga,
with shovels, plastic sheets and watering cans around its edges. [@1] No growers
were present when the police arrived. [@1] The head of Swaziland's anti-drug
unit, Supt Albert Mkhatshwa, accompanied the search-and-destroy operation and
said such plantations were nothing out of the ordinary. [@1] Police said they
would spray the crop with weed killer so the plants would be dead within a day
or so. [@1]

Swazi anti-drug unit statistics for the first seven months of 2006 recorded
2,407 kg of compressed cannabis seized. [@1] The same statistics recorded a
further 356.5 hectares of cannabis plantations destroyed. [@1] Mkhatshwa said
his units now go into dense bush and mountainous areas on foot with weed killer
and spray every two weeks, but it is not having much impact. [@1] South Africa
recently withdrew assistance such as helicopters and off-road vehicles, leaving
Swazi police with meagre resources. [@1] South African Police spokesperson Ronan
Naidoo said cooperation on operations was no longer in place because of
escalating costs. [@1]

Once compressed and packaged, consignments are taken into neighbouring countries
using back roads or holes cut in the border fence, which Mkhatshwa called
notoriously difficult to monitor. [@1] South Africa's Institute for Security
Studies said the best-quality harvest is compressed into one- or two-kilogram
blocks smuggled via South Africa and Mozambique to Europe and the UK. [@1] The
institute found growers received between $45 and $52 per kilogram, while
high-quality Swazi Gold fetched as much as $7,600 per kilogram in the UK. [@1]
The institute added that high-quality Swazi Gold fetched as much as $7,600 a
kilogram in the UK. [@1]
