Customs officers and judicial police searched a commercial vessel from Europe
carrying several thousand vehicles for the Caribbean when it docked at the port
of Fort-de-France on the morning of Monday 3 August 2015. [@1] About twenty
agents took part, including several customs detector-dog teams, and the check
covered all of the more than 600 cars due for import into Martinique. [@1,5] After
several hours of checks the agents found thirteen "valises marocaines" —
Moroccan-style hashish blocks — of about 30 kg each lying on the floor at the
back of a utility van. [@1,4,5]

The resin weighed 404 kilos in total and was seized immediately. [@1,4,5] Customs
put the market value of the shipment at more than 2.4 million euros. [@1,5]
Customs called it the largest cannabis resin seizure ever made in the whole of
the French Antilles. [@1,5] France-Antilles reported that the resin had been
loaded into a new vehicle at the port of Le Havre and found at Fort-de-France,
and that more than 800,000 euros in cash had also been found on passengers at
the airport since July. [@1] franceinfo gave the same value, more than 2.4
million euros, and attributed the record claim to customs. [@3]

The operation was prepared by the OCRTIS, France's central office against
illicit drug trafficking, and the DNRED, the customs intelligence and
investigation directorate, as reinforced checks on commercial freight. [@1,4] On
the instructions of the Fort-de-France prosecutor, the investigation, followed
by the specialised interregional court (JIRS), was assigned to the OCRTIS
Caribbean branch. [@1] RCI reported that the drugs were in bags, almost "thrown"
into the van rather than hidden, and that a tip let officers find the cargo
among the 600 cars. [@2] RCI also reported that the officers were helped by dogs
and found the load among 600 cars after receiving intelligence. [@2]

Fort-de-France prosecutor Éric Corbeaux said there had been no arrests at that
stage and that investigations were under way in Martinique and in mainland
France. [@2] He said the traffickers used a "rip off" technique, placing the
goods without the knowledge of the vehicle's owner or of the dealer delivering
the cars. [@2] He added that the resin, which is not produced in the Caribbean,
serves as currency in exchange for cocaine. [@2] He said two kilos of resin,
relatively cheap in mainland France, buy one kilo of cocaine that is then resold
at retail in France at an enormous profit. [@2]

The van holding the resin was a Mercedes utility vehicle. [@4] In mainland France, about 8 kg of cannabis resin is needed to obtain 1 kg of cocaine. [@4]
