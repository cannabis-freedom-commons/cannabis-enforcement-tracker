The Royal Swaziland Police Department runs an ongoing operation that destroys
marijuana grown for commercial purposes. [@1] Marijuana valued at nearly $1
million was burned in a recent police operation. [@1] The UN Office on Drugs and
Crime said Swazi eradication is expensive because cultivation happens mostly in
remote, hard-to-access areas and the law-enforcement agencies do not have
sufficient capacity. [@1] The UNODC regional spokesperson Andreas Zeidler said
the police do regularly seize large amounts of cannabis and arrest suspected
traffickers inside the country and at its borders. [@1]

For 75-year-old Gogo Thwala, who looks after six grandchildren, selling cannabis
that grows around her mud-and-stick house is how she buys food. [@1] She said
police had come and she told them that, as an old woman, she could not look
after her garden. [@1] Her cannabis garden, known locally as dagga or insangu,
stretches over a half-acre plot between maize plants, trees and boulders, with
some plants over 2m high. [@1] Larger fields belonging to neighbours are hidden
in mountain crevices, making them hard to detect when law enforcers make their
rare inspection tours. [@1]

Most of the crop is sold on the domestic market and in South Africa, where a
small packet sells for US$2 on the streets, while the best quality is compressed
into one- or two-kilogram blocks and smuggled through South Africa and
Mozambique to Europe. [@1] The government is not considering legalising
marijuana and has not examined whether cannabis or hemp could become an
economically viable crop. [@1] A Ministry of Justice source said Swaziland is a
signatory to international drug accords and has to discourage trafficking of
drugs grown in the country from crossing the border. [@1] A South African
institute's review of regional drug strategies found interception, seizure and
border interdiction had shown limited or low success rates, with enforcement
focused on low-level dealers, consumers and couriers. [@1]
